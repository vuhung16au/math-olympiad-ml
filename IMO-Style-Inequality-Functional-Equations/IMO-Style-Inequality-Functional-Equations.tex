\section*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Preface}

This is a growing booklet of olympiad-style functional equations and
inequalities. It is written especially for students in Years 10--12 preparing
for mathematical olympiads, as well as for anyone who enjoys practising with
challenging, unusual, and unfamiliar problems. The aim is to develop flexible
problem-solving habits, not just to collect answers.

The collection includes problems with different domains, methods, and levels
of rigidity. Some problems have more than one solution, while others admit a
parameter family or an exceptional value until the final hypotheses are used.

\section*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}

The problems focus on olympiad-style functional equations built around
inequalities. Their domains include the positive real numbers, the real
numbers, the positive rationals, and the positive integers. These domains
require different tools: order and limits on the positive reals, symmetry and
regularity on the real line, prime exponents on the positive rationals, and
counting or divisibility on the integers. The booklet is arranged in five
themed parts: additive and multiplicative rigidity; reciprocal and harmonic
transforms; iteration, fixed points, and growth; mean and norm squeezes; and
real-line and polynomial inequalities. Problems come first, grouped by theme,
and the full solutions appear together at the end.

For each problem, first read the statement carefully and try to solve it on
your own. Pay attention to the domain and to what the inequalities say for
special choices of inputs. If you get stuck, use the hint as a nudge rather
than as a replacement for a proof. Once you have a solution, compare it with

the one at the end: check each step, note any idea that you did not use, and
compare alternative solutions when they are provided.

\section*{Notations}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Notations}

Only the booklet-specific conventions are listed here; standard olympiad
notation is left implicit.

\paragraph{Domains.}
$\mathbb R$, $\mathbb Q$, and $\mathbb Z$ denote the real, rational, and
integer numbers. A subscript $>0$ means positive elements, while a subscript
$\ge0$ means nonnegative elements; for example,
$\mathbb Q_{>0}$ and $\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$. A function such as
$f:\mathbb Q\to[0,\infty)$ has exactly the displayed domain and codomain.

\paragraph{Iterates and auxiliary functions.}
$f^{\circ n}$ denotes the $n$-fold iterate of $f$, not the pointwise power
$f^n$. When a candidate model $M$ is known, we may write
$g(x)=f(x)-M(x)$ for its defect, or $f(x)/M(x)$ for its normalized ratio
when $M(x)>0$.

\paragraph{Discrete approximations.}
$\lfloor t\rfloor$ and $\lceil t\rceil$ are the floor and ceiling of $t$.
The term \emph{dyadic} means having denominator a power of $2$ (for example,
$m/2^n$); dyadic numbers are used as a dense subset of the real line.

\paragraph{Prime exponents.}
For a prime $p$, $v_p(x)$ denotes the exponent of $p$ in a nonzero rational
$x$, allowing negative exponents. Thus
\[
v_p(xy)=v_p(x)+v_p(y).
\]

\section*{Fundamental Review: Facts and Tools}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Fundamental Review: Facts and Tools}

The \emph{squeeze principle} says that if a quantity is bounded above and
below by the same value, it must equal that value. In these problems, one
inequality often supplies an upper bound and another a matching lower bound,
sometimes only after a substitution or iteration.

\paragraph{Order and monotonicity.}
Adding inequalities preserves their direction. Multiplying or dividing by a
positive quantity also preserves the direction, while doing so by a negative
quantity reverses it. Applying an increasing function preserves order; for
positive numbers, taking reciprocals reverses order. A function is
\emph{nondecreasing} if $x\le y$ implies $f(x)\le f(y)$. For example, a
positive superadditive function on the positive reals is strictly increasing:
if $0<x<y$, then $f(y)\ge f(x)+f(y-x)>f(x)$.

\paragraph{Means.}
For nonnegative $u,v$, the arithmetic--geometric mean inequality is
\[
\frac{u+v}{2}\ge\sqrt{uv},
\]
with equality exactly when $u=v$. The root-mean-square--arithmetic-mean
inequality is
\[
\sqrt{\frac{u^2+v^2}{2}}\ge\frac{u+v}{2}
\qquad (u,v\ge0),
\]
again with equality exactly when $u=v$. These inequalities are useful when
the problem places a function expression between two familiar means.

\paragraph{Iteration and approximation.}
Mathematical induction justifies repeated addition or multiplication, such as
deriving bounds at integer multiples or powers. The Archimedean property says
that the positive integers are unbounded. In particular, for $t>0$,
\[
0\le t-\frac{\lfloor nt\rfloor}{n}<\frac1n,
\]
so $\lfloor nt\rfloor/n\to t$. Floor approximations of this kind let us
compare a value at an arbitrary real input with values at integer multiples.

\paragraph{Density and monotonicity.}
The rational numbers are dense in the real numbers, and
$\{2^q:q\in\mathbb Q\}$ is dense in the positive reals. If a nondecreasing
function agrees with the identity on a dense subset of its domain, it agrees
with the identity throughout: nearby points from the dense subset bound its
value from below and above.

\paragraph{Defects and normalization.}
When a candidate model $M(x)$ is suggested by the problem, it is often useful
to study the defect
\[
g(x)=f(x)-M(x)
\]
or the ratio $f(x)/M(x)$ when $M(x)>0$. The hypotheses may become simpler in
terms of $g$: a polynomial expression may become a midpoint inequality, while
a multiplicative expression may become submultiplicative after taking
reciprocals or logarithms. Always check that the normalization is defined on
the stated domain.

\paragraph{Iteration and bounded growth.}
If a relation is applied repeatedly, write the first few cases and identify
the resulting sequence. A nonnegative quantity cannot have a negative partial
sum, a bounded quantity cannot grow exponentially, and a quantity bounded by
$C/2^n$ for every $n$ must be zero. These elementary observations often turn
an inequality into an identity.

\paragraph{Discrete rigidity.}
On the positive integers, injectivity permits counting arguments: $N$
distinct inputs require $N$ distinct positive outputs. On the positive
rationals, unique factorization permits comparison of prime exponents and
divisibility chains. These tools are different from real-variable arguments,
where order, density, and regularity are central.

\paragraph{Midpoints and quadratic identities.}
The parallelogram identity
\[
\frac{(x+y)^2+(x-y)^2}{2}=x^2+y^2
\]
often simplifies inequalities on the real line. A related midpoint fact is
that a monotone function on an interval satisfying
\[
f\!\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)=\frac{f(x)+f(y)}2
\]
for all $x,y$ in that interval is affine. This is a regularity result for the midpoint Jensen
equation; no continuity assumption needs to be added when monotonicity is
known.

Basic algebra is used throughout: factor differences of squares, use the laws
of exponents, and handle reciprocals carefully, noting that reciprocal order
reversal requires positive quantities. Also choose inputs such as $x=y$,
$y=1$, or $y=-x$ only when the domain permits them. Normalizing a function,
for example by setting $g(x)=f(x)/x^k$, can expose simpler behavior; always
check that the normalization is defined on the stated domain. When a formula
is first established on the integers, rationals, or dyadic rationals, do not
assume that it extends automatically to all real inputs: first prove the
continuity, monotonicity, boundedness, or other regularity needed for that
extension.

\section*{Fundamental Review: Core Theorems}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Fundamental Review: Core Theorems}

The following facts appear repeatedly in olympiad-style functional
inequalities. Use them only when their hypotheses have been verified.

\paragraph{1. Squeezing and equality.}
If $A\le B\le A$, then $A=B$. More generally, if

\[
0\le u_n\le v_n\qquad\text{and}\qquad v_n\to0,
\]
then $u_n\to0$. One inequality often gives one side of a bound, while a
reciprocal substitution, symmetry, or iteration gives the other side. Always
record the equality condition in a standard inequality; it often identifies
the candidate function.

\paragraph{2. The basic mean inequalities.}
For $u,v\ge0$,
\[
\sqrt{uv}\le \frac{u+v}{2}
\]
with equality if and only if $u=v$, and
\[
\frac{u+v}{2}\le
\sqrt{\frac{u^2+v^2}{2}}
\]
with equality if and only if $u=v$. The Cauchy--Schwarz inequality is
\[
\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i b_i\right)^2
\le
\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^2\right)
\left(\sum_{i=1}^n b_i^2\right),
\]
including the useful special case $2uv\le u^2+v^2$.

\paragraph{3. Triangle and norm inequalities.}
For real numbers,
\[
|x+y|\le |x|+|y|.
\]
Consequently, for $0\le\alpha\le1$,
\[
|x+y|^\alpha\le |x|^\alpha+|y|^\alpha.
\]
The Euclidean norm satisfies the triangle inequality as well:
\[
\sqrt{\sum_i(x_i+y_i)^2}
\le \sqrt{\sum_i x_i^2}+\sqrt{\sum_i y_i^2}.
\]

\paragraph{4. Induction, iteration, and growth.}
Repeated application is justified by induction. For example,
\[
g(x+y)\ge g(x)+g(y)\Longrightarrow g(nx)\ge ng(x),
\]
and
\[
g(xy)\le g(x)g(y)\Longrightarrow g(x^n)\le g(x)^n.
\]
A bounded quantity cannot grow exponentially. If a nonnegative quantity is
bounded above by $C/r^n$ for every $n$, where $r>1$, it is zero.

\paragraph{5. Archimedean approximation.}
The positive integers are unbounded, and for $t>0$,
\[
0\le t-\frac{\lfloor nt\rfloor}{n}<\frac1n,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\frac{\lfloor nt\rfloor}{n}\longrightarrow t.
\]
Floor and ceiling approximations transfer conclusions from integer inputs to
arbitrary real inputs, usually after monotonicity has been proved.

\paragraph{6. Monotonicity and density.}
If $x\le y$ implies $f(x)\le f(y)$, then $f$ is nondecreasing. A positive
superadditive function on $(0,\infty)$ is strictly increasing, since
\[
0<x<y\Longrightarrow f(y)\ge f(x)+f(y-x)>f(x).
\]
The rationals are dense in $\mathbb R$, and
$\{2^q:q\in\mathbb Q\}$ is dense in $(0,\infty)$. A nondecreasing function
that agrees with a known function on a dense subset agrees with it everywhere.

\paragraph{7. Midpoint and additive rigidity.}
If a monotone function satisfies
\[
f\!\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)=\frac{f(x)+f(y)}2,
\]
then it is affine. An additive function satisfying
$f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)$ is linear once one proves a regularity condition such as
monotonicity, boundedness on an interval, or continuity at one point.
Additivity alone does not justify continuity.

\textbf{Continuity is proved, not assumed, in this booklet}, especially in
the additive, quadratic, and midpoint-identity problems.

\paragraph{8. Quadratic identities.}
The parallelogram identity is
\[
f(x+y)+f(x-y)=2f(x)+2f(y).
\]
For $f(x)=x^2$ this follows from
\[
(x+y)^2+(x-y)^2=2x^2+2y^2.
\]
A one-sided inequality can become this identity after symmetry supplies the
reverse inequality. A separately proved regularity condition is still needed
to rule out pathological solutions on the real line.

\paragraph{9. Integer and rational rigidity.}
Unique prime factorization gives
\[
n=\prod_p p^{v_p(n)}
\]
for positive integers, with integer exponents; for nonzero positive rationals,
the exponents may be negative. Multiplicative problems can therefore be
studied prime by prime. Bézout's identity says that if $\gcd(a,b)=1$, then
there exist integers $r,s$ such that $ra+sb=1$.

On the positive integers, injectivity gives a counting principle: $N$
distinct inputs require $N$ distinct positive outputs. This can contradict an
upper bound placing all outputs in a set with fewer than $N$ elements.

\section*{Frequently Seen Patterns}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Frequently Seen Patterns}

Look for special inputs that produce bounds, identities, or fixed points, then
combine or repeat those consequences until they become decisive. The useful
substitutions depend on the domain: try $x=y$, $y=1$, $y=-x$, $x=0$, $x=1$,
reciprocal inputs, scaled inputs, and values such as $x=f(y)$ or $x=f(x)$
when these are permitted.

\paragraph{Normalize the expected answer.}
If the statement contains products, reciprocals, roots, powers, or a familiar
polynomial expression, introduce a transformed function. Useful examples are
\[
g(x)=\frac1{f(x)},\qquad g(x)=\sqrt{f(x)},\qquad
g(x)=f(x)-x^2,\qquad g(x)=\frac{f(x)}x,\qquad g(x)=\log f(x).
\]
A good normalization can turn a complicated inequality into superadditivity,
subadditivity, submultiplicativity, midpoint concavity, or a statement about
a nonnegative defect.

\paragraph{Track and eliminate a defect.}
After identifying a likely model $M(x)$, define $g(x)=f(x)-M(x)$ or
$g(x)=f(x)/M(x)$. If a positive defect grows under translation, scaling,
multiplication, or iteration while another condition keeps it bounded, the
defect must vanish. Conversely, if repeated scaling bounds it by a quantity
tending to zero, it must be zero.

\paragraph{Reverse inequalities and propagate equality.}
Interchanging variables, reversing an arithmetic progression, or applying a
condition at reciprocal inputs can produce the opposite inequality. Together
the two directions force equality. A single anchor value can then propagate
through integer multiples, powers, roots, dyadic scaling, or density. Equality
cases in standard mean and norm inequalities are especially informative.

\paragraph{Use iteration and composition.}
A pointwise estimate for $f$ is often most effective after evaluating it at
$f(x)$. Compare that result with a condition on $f(f(x))$ or a higher iterate
to eliminate constants, force fixed points, or rule out an alternative branch.

\paragraph{Use the domain's arithmetic structure.}
On the positive integers, count distinct values and use divisibility. On the
positive rationals, track prime supports and prime exponents under iteration.
On the positive reals, use order, reciprocal reversal, roots, and density. On
the full real line, exploit signs and symmetry, but prove any needed
monotonicity or continuity rather than assuming it.

\paragraph{Prove regularity before extending a formula.}
Many real-line solutions first determine a function on integers, rationals, or
dyadic rationals. That information alone is not enough. Establish continuity,
monotonicity, boundedness, or a Lipschitz estimate from the hypotheses before
passing from a dense subset to all real inputs. In particular, continuity is
proved, not assumed, in additive, quadratic, and midpoint-identity arguments.

\paragraph{Keep equality cases and exceptional parameters.}
After finding a candidate, substitute it into every original condition and
record where equality occurs. Do not discard zero slopes, zero exponents,
constant solutions, endpoint values, or exceptional values at the origin
without checking whether the domain and hypotheses genuinely exclude them.

\section*{Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Conclusion}

The central lesson of these problems is to keep experimenting with the
inequalities: choose useful inputs, normalize the function when appropriate,
look for quantities to compare, and follow a promising bound until it becomes
decisive. Symmetry, reciprocal substitutions, iteration, density, and
discrete arithmetic can all turn a weak-looking inequality into a rigid
identity.

Not every problem ends with the same kind of conclusion. Some force a unique
function, some leave a parameter family, and some require a separate analysis
of exceptional points or equality cases. In every case, respect the domain,
prove continuity or other regularity rather than assuming it, verify the final
candidates in the original statement, and use failed approaches as information
about the hidden structure of the problem. We hope this booklet encourages you
to approach unfamiliar problems with patience, curiosity, and confidence.
