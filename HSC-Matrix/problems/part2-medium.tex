

\begin{problem}[Matrix Systems and Polynomial Curve Fitting]
A quadratic function has a rule of the form $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$. It is known that the points $(-1, 12)$ and $(1, 4)$ are on the graph of $f$, alongside a third point $(2, 9)$.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
    \item By substituting the known points, write down three linear equations satisfied by $a, b$, and $c$. Formulate this information as a matrix equation of the form $M \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{k}$, where $\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \end{bmatrix}$.
    \item The inverse of the coefficient matrix $M$ is given by $M^{-1} = \frac{1}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -3 & 2 \\ -3 & 3 & 0 \\ 2 & 6 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$. Use this inverse to solve the matrix equation and determine the exact values of $a, b$, and $c$.
    \item Suppose the $y$-coordinate of the third point is changed to a parameter $p$, such that the point is now $(2, p)$. Write down the new constant vector $\mathbf{k}$, and use $M^{-1}$ to determine the coefficients $a, b$, and $c$ in terms of $p$.
    \item Determine the specific value of $p$ for which the quadratic function degenerates into a linear function (i.e., $a = 0$). Geometrically, what does this imply about the arrangement of the three points on the Cartesian plane?
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{hint}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Part (a):} Substitute the $x$ and $y$ values of each coordinate pair into the quadratic equation to generate three distinct linear equations. The coefficients of $a, b,$ and $c$ will form your $3 \times 3$ matrix $M$.
    \item \textbf{Part (b):} To solve $M \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{k}$, pre-multiply both sides by the inverse matrix so that $\mathbf{v} = M^{-1} \mathbf{k}$.
    \item \textbf{Part (c):} Update the third entry of your $\mathbf{k}$ vector to $p$. Multiply the matrix $M^{-1}$ by this new vector using standard row-by-column multiplication.
    \item \textbf{Part (d):} Take your parametric expression for $a$ from part (c) and set it equal to 0. If a quadratic curve becomes a straight line, consider what that means for the points that define it.
\end{itemize}
\end{hint}

\begin{solution}
\textbf{(a)} \\
Substituting $(-1, 12)$: $a(-1)^2 + b(-1) + c = 12 \implies a - b + c = 12$ \\
Substituting $(1, 4)$: $a(1)^2 + b(1) + c = 4 \implies a + b + c = 4$ \\
Substituting $(2, 9)$: $a(2)^2 + b(2) + c = 9 \implies 4a + 2b + c = 9$ \\
Matrix equation: $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 \\ 4 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix}$.

\medskip
\textbf{(b)} \\
$\mathbf{v} = M^{-1} \mathbf{k} = \frac{1}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -3 & 2 \\ -3 & 3 & 0 \\ 2 & 6 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 12 \\ 4 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix}$ \\
$a = \frac{1}{6} (1(12) - 3(4) + 2(9)) = \frac{1}{6}(12 - 12 + 18) = 3$ \\
$b = \frac{1}{6} (-3(12) + 3(4) + 0(9)) = \frac{1}{6}(-36 + 12) = -4$ \\
$c = \frac{1}{6} (2(12) + 6(4) - 2(9)) = \frac{1}{6}(24 + 24 - 18) = 5$ \\
Therefore, $a = 3$, $b = -4$, and $c = 5$.

\medskip
\textbf{(c)} \\
With the new point $(2, p)$, the constant vector becomes $\mathbf{k} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 \\ 4 \\ p \end{bmatrix}$. \\
$\mathbf{v} = \frac{1}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -3 & 2 \\ -3 & 3 & 0 \\ 2 & 6 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 12 \\ 4 \\ p \end{bmatrix}$ \\
$a = \frac{1}{6} (12 - 12 + 2p) = \frac{2p}{6} = \frac{p}{3}$ \\
$b = \frac{1}{6} (-36 + 12 + 0) = -4$ \\
$c = \frac{1}{6} (24 + 24 - 2p) = \frac{48 - 2p}{6} = 8 - \frac{p}{3}$.

\medskip
\textbf{(d)} \\
For the function to be linear, $a = 0$. \\
$\frac{p}{3} = 0 \implies p = 0$. \\
If $a = 0$, the curve is a straight line $y = -4x + 8$. Geometrically, this implies that the three points $(-1, 12)$, $(1, 4)$, and $(2, 0)$ are perfectly collinear.
\end{solution}

\begin{takeaways}
\item \textbf{Vandermonde Matrices:} The coefficient matrix formed by fitting a polynomial to coordinate points is known as a Vandermonde matrix. Its determinant is always non-zero as long as the $x$-coordinates are distinct, guaranteeing a unique polynomial fit.
\item \textbf{Parametric Matrix Operations:} Treating unknowns as parameters (like $p$) within a matrix equation allows you to generalize solutions for an entire family of curves at once, rather than re-solving the system from scratch.
\item \textbf{Geometric Limits:} Setting the leading coefficient of a polynomial to zero algebraically represents flattening the curve's curvature, visually forcing the defining points into a straight line.
\end{takeaways}


\begin{problem}[Matrix Systems and Intersecting Planes]
Consider the following system of homogeneous linear equations, which represent three planes passing through the origin:
\begin{align*}
x - y - z &= 0 \\
2x + 4y - z &= 0 \\
3x + 3y + kz &= 0
\end{align*}
where $k$ is a real constant.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
    \item Write the system in the matrix form $AX = O$, where $O$ is the zero vector. Find the determinant of the coefficient matrix $A$ in terms of $k$, and determine the specific value of $k$ for which $A$ is a singular matrix.
    \item For the singular value of $k$ found in part (a), show algebraically that the system is consistent. Let $y = -t$ and solve the simultaneous equations for $x$ and $z$ strictly in terms of the parameter $t$.
    \item Hence, write the vector equation of the line of intersection of these planes in the form $\mathbf{r} = t(a\mathbf{i} + b\mathbf{j} + c\mathbf{k})$. Briefly explain how this line relates to the intersection of just the first two planes in the system.
    \item If $k = 4$, determine the unique solution to the system algebraically, and explain what this result means about the geometric arrangement of the three planes.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{hint}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Part (a):} The coefficient matrix $A$ consists of the multipliers of $x, y,$ and $z$. Calculate the determinant of this $3 \times 3$ matrix by expanding across the top row. A matrix is singular when its determinant is exactly zero.
    \item \textbf{Part (b):} Substitute the value of $k$ into the third equation. To prove consistency, look for a linear combination of the first two equations (e.g., adding them together) that perfectly matches the third equation. Then, substitute $y = -t$ into the first two equations and solve the resulting system for $x$ and $z$.
    \item \textbf{Part (c):} Construct a position vector $\mathbf{r} = x\mathbf{i} + y\mathbf{j} + z\mathbf{k}$ using your parametric expressions from part (b), and factor out $t$. 
    \item \textbf{Part (d):} If $k = 4$, calculate $\det(A)$ to confirm it is non-zero (meaning the matrix is invertible). Since the constant vector is the zero vector, multiplying it by any inverse matrix will always yield the zero vector. 
\end{itemize}
\end{hint}

\begin{solution}
\textbf{(a)} \\
The matrix equation is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & -1 \\ 2 & 4 & -1 \\ 3 & 3 & k \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. \\
$\det(A) = 1(4k - (-3)) - (-1)(2k - (-3)) + (-1)(6 - 12)$ \\
$\det(A) = (4k + 3) + (2k + 3) - (-6) = 6k + 12$. \\
For $A$ to be singular, $\det(A) = 0 \implies 6k + 12 = 0 \implies k = -2$.

\medskip
\textbf{(b)} \\
Substitute $k = -2$: the third equation is $3x + 3y - 2z = 0$. \\
Adding Equation 1 and Equation 2 gives: \\
$(x - y - z) + (2x + 4y - z) = 3x + 3y - 2z = 0$. \\
Since this perfectly matches the third equation, the system is consistent (the third plane is dependent on the first two). \\
Let $y = -t$. \\
Equation 1 becomes: $x - (-t) - z = 0 \implies x - z = -t$. \\
Equation 2 becomes: $2x + 4(-t) - z = 0 \implies 2x - z = 4t$. \\
Subtracting Equation 1 from Equation 2: $(2x - z) - (x - z) = 4t - (-t) \implies x = 5t$. \\
Substituting $x$ back into Equation 1: $5t - z = -t \implies z = 6t$. \\
The solution is $x = 5t, y = -t, z = 6t$.

\medskip
\textbf{(c)} \\
The vector equation is $\mathbf{r} = 5t\mathbf{i} - t\mathbf{j} + 6t\mathbf{k} = t(5\mathbf{i} - \mathbf{j} + 6\mathbf{k})$. \\
Because the third plane is simply a linear combination of the first two, it contains their line of intersection. Therefore, this vector equation represents the straight line intersection of the planes $x - y - z = 0$ and $2x + 4y - z = 0$ through the origin.

\medskip
\textbf{(d)} \\
If $k = 4$, $\det(A) = 6(4) + 12 = 36$. \\
Since $\det(A) \neq 0$, matrix $A$ is invertible. \\
$X = A^{-1}O = O \implies x = 0, y = 0, z = 0$. \\
Geometrically, this means the three planes intersect at a single, unique point, which is the origin $(0,0,0)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{takeaways}
\item \textbf{Homogeneous Systems:} A system where all constants are zero always has at least one solution (the trivial solution: the origin). Whether it has \textit{infinite} solutions depends on singularity.
\item \textbf{Singularity and Intersection:} If the determinant of a $3 \times 3$ coefficient matrix is zero, the planes form a dependent system. If consistent, they intersect along a common line (forming a sheaf of planes).
\item \textbf{Parametric Conversions:} Expressing an infinite solution set algebraically using a parameter ($t$) maps directly to the Cartesian components of a 3D vector line equation.
\end{takeaways}


\begin{problem}[Leslie Matrices and Parametric Population Modeling]
Based on standard Leslie matrix population modeling principles, consider a modified species of female insect with a maximum life span of 3 months. The population is divided into three age groups of 1 month each. 

The Leslie matrix, $L$, for this population is given by:
$$L = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & k & 4 \\ 0.5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
where $k$ represents the unknown fecundity (birth rate) of the second age group. Assume that each of the three age groups initially consists of 400 females, represented by the initial population state matrix $P_0$.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
    \item Express the population column matrix after 1 month, $P_1$, strictly in terms of $k$.
    \item Given that the total population of female insects after 1 month is exactly $2680$, algebraically determine the value of $k$.
    \item Using your value of $k$ from part (b), an environmental pesticide is introduced that immediately halves the survival rates of all age groups moving forward. Construct the new Leslie matrix, $L_{\text{new}}$, and calculate the exact population matrix for month 2, $P_2$.
    \item Evaluate the determinant of the original matrix $L$. Deduce whether the original population transition model is mathematically reversible (invertible), and algebraically explain why the value of the determinant is completely independent of the fecundity parameter $k$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{hint}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Part (a):} The initial population matrix is a $3 \times 1$ column vector containing the value 400 in each row. Multiply the $3 \times 3$ Leslie matrix by this vector using standard row-by-column multiplication.
    \item \textbf{Part (b):} Sum the three row elements of your $P_1$ matrix to find an expression for the total population in terms of $k$. Set this expression equal to 2680 and solve the resulting linear equation.
    \item \textbf{Part (c):} The survival rates are located on the sub-diagonal of the Leslie matrix (the $0.5$ and $0.2$). Divide these by 2 to create $L_{\text{new}}$. To find $P_2$, multiply $L_{\text{new}}$ by the numerical $P_1$ matrix you found in part (b).
    \item \textbf{Part (d):} Calculate the determinant of the $3 \times 3$ matrix $L$ by expanding along the first column or the third row, as they contain multiple zeros. A matrix model is reversible (has an inverse) if its determinant is non-zero.
\end{itemize}
\end{hint}

\begin{solution}
\textbf{(a)} \\
$P_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 400 \\ 400 \\ 400 \end{bmatrix}$ \\
$P_1 = L P_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & k & 4 \\ 0.5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.2 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 400 \\ 400 \\ 400 \end{bmatrix}$ \\
$P_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0(400) + k(400) + 4(400) \\ 0.5(400) + 0 + 0 \\ 0 + 0.2(400) + 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 400k + 1600 \\ 200 \\ 80 \end{bmatrix}$.

\medskip
\textbf{(b)} \\
The total population is the sum of the elements in $P_1$: \\
Total $= (400k + 1600) + 200 + 80 = 400k + 1880$. \\
We are given that the total population is 2680. \\
$400k + 1880 = 2680 \implies 400k = 800 \implies k = 2$.

\medskip
\textbf{(c)} \\
With $k = 2$, $P_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2400 \\ 200 \\ 80 \end{bmatrix}$. \\
The pesticide halves the survival rates ($0.5 \to 0.25$ and $0.2 \to 0.1$). The fecundities in the top row remain unchanged. \\
$L_{\text{new}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & 4 \\ 0.25 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ \\
$P_2 = L_{\text{new}} P_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & 4 \\ 0.25 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2400 \\ 200 \\ 80 \end{bmatrix}$ \\
$P_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 + 2(200) + 4(80) \\ 0.25(2400) + 0 + 0 \\ 0 + 0.1(200) + 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 400 + 320 \\ 600 \\ 20 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 720 \\ 600 \\ 20 \end{bmatrix}$. \\
After 2 months, there are 720 insects in age group 1, 600 in age group 2, and 20 in age group 3.

\medskip
\textbf{(d)} \\
Expanding the determinant of $L$ along the first column: \\
$\det(L) = 0 - 0.5 \det\begin{bmatrix} k & 4 \\ 0.2 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + 0$ \\
$\det(L) = -0.5 \left( (k)(0) - (4)(0.2) \right) = -0.5(0 - 0.8) = 0.4$. \\
Since $\det(L) = 0.4 \neq 0$, the matrix $L$ is invertible, meaning the population transition can be mathematically reversed to find past population states. \\
The value of $k$ disappears during the determinant calculation because expanding the $2 \times 2$ minor matrix forces $k$ to be multiplied by $0$ (the bottom right element of the Leslie matrix). In a standard Leslie matrix, the determinant depends strictly on the product of the survival rates and the final age group's fecundity.
\end{solution}

\begin{takeaways}
\item \textbf{Parametric Modeling:} Using matrices to solve for unknown biological parameters (like fecundity $k$) demonstrates how models are calibrated using empirical field data (e.g., total population counts).
\item \textbf{Dynamic Matrices:} Real-world matrix models are rarely static. Understanding how to manually manipulate specific elements (like survival rates) allows for the simulation of environmental impacts over discrete time steps.
\item \textbf{Biological Meaning of Determinants:} The algebraic structure of a Leslie matrix dictates that its determinant is independent of intermediate birth rates. A singular Leslie matrix (determinant of zero) only occurs if an age group has a zero survival rate or if the oldest age group becomes sterile, mathematically erasing historical population data.
\end{takeaways}


\begin{problem}[Leslie Matrices and Stable Populations]
Consider a certain species of fish divided into three age groups, each one year in length. The fish do not reproduce in their first year, produce an average of $1.5$ offspring in their second year, and produce an unknown average of $k$ offspring in their third year, after which they die. Assume that $40\%$ of the fish survive their first year, and $50\%$ of those survivors make it to their third year. The initial population consists of $1000$ newborns and zero older fish.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
    \item Construct the Leslie matrix, $L$, for this fish population and calculate the population state column matrix after 2 years, $P_2$.
    \item Determine an algebraic expression for the number of newborns present in year 3 in terms of $k$.
    \item For the population to eventually reach a strict long-term stable size (a long-term growth rate of exactly $1$), determine the required exact value of the parameter $k$.
    \item Using the value of $k$ determined in part (c), find the long-term stable age distribution of the fish population, expressing the number of fish in each age group as a simplified fraction of the total population.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{hint}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Part (a):} The Leslie matrix is a $3 \times 3$ matrix where the first row contains the fecundities (birth rates) and the sub-diagonal contains the survival rates. To find $P_2$, multiply the initial population column matrix by $L$ twice (or by $L^2$).
    \item \textbf{Part (b):} Multiply your result for $P_2$ by the Leslie matrix $L$ to find $P_3$. The top element of this resulting matrix represents the newborns.
    \item \textbf{Part (c):} A long-term growth rate of $1$ means the population state eventually satisfies the equation $L P = P$, where $P = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}$. Expand this matrix equation into a system of simultaneous linear equations and solve for $k$.
    \item \textbf{Part (d):} Using the system of equations from part (c), express the number of fish in each age group ($x$, $y$, and $z$) in terms of a single variable, such as $y$. Sum these to find the total population, and then write each age group as a fraction of that total.
\end{itemize}
\end{hint}

\begin{solution}
\textbf{(a)} \\
The Leslie matrix is $L = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1.5 & k \\ 0.4 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.5 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. \\
The initial population is $P_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 1000 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. \\
$P_1 = L P_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1.5 & k \\ 0.4 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.5 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1000 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 400 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. \\
$P_2 = L P_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1.5 & k \\ 0.4 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.5 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 400 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 600 \\ 0 \\ 200 \end{bmatrix}$.

\medskip
\textbf{(b)} \\
$P_3 = L P_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1.5 & k \\ 0.4 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.5 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 600 \\ 0 \\ 200 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0(600) + 1.5(0) + k(200) \\ 0.4(600) + 0 + 0 \\ 0 + 0.5(0) + 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 200k \\ 240 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. \\
The number of newborns in year 3 is $200k$.

\medskip
\textbf{(c)} \\
For a long-term growth rate of $1$, the stable state satisfies $L P = P$: \\
$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1.5 & k \\ 0.4 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.5 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}$ \\
This yields the system of equations: \\
(1) $1.5y + kz = x$ \\
(2) $0.4x = y \implies x = 2.5y$ \\
(3) $0.5y = z$ \\
Substitute (2) and (3) into (1): \\
$1.5y + k(0.5y) = 2.5y$ \\
Assuming a non-zero population, divide by $y$: \\
$1.5 + 0.5k = 2.5 \implies 0.5k = 1 \implies k = 2$.

\medskip
\textbf{(d)} \\
With $k = 2$, the age group relations are $x = 2.5y$ and $z = 0.5y$. \\
Total population $T = x + y + z = 2.5y + y + 0.5y = 4y$. \\
Proportion of age group 1: $\frac{x}{T} = \frac{2.5y}{4y} = \frac{2.5}{4} = \frac{5}{8}$. \\
Proportion of age group 2: $\frac{y}{T} = \frac{1y}{4y} = \frac{1}{4} = \frac{2}{8}$. \\
Proportion of age group 3: $\frac{z}{T} = \frac{0.5y}{4y} = \frac{0.5}{4} = \frac{1}{8}$. \\
The stable age distribution is $\frac{5}{8}$ newborns, $\frac{1}{4}$ one-year-olds, and $\frac{1}{8}$ two-year-olds.
\end{solution}

\begin{takeaways}
\item \textbf{Eigenvalues in Context:} A long-term growth rate of $1$ implies that the dominant eigenvalue of the Leslie matrix is $\lambda = 1$. Setting $LP = 1P$ allows you to bypass the characteristic polynomial and directly solve for matrix parameters.
\item \textbf{Stable Age Distributions:} Once a population reaches its long-term growth trajectory (determined by the dominant eigenvalue), the \textit{ratio} of the age groups remains constant year over year, representing the corresponding eigenvector.
\item \textbf{Matrix Generality:} Using variables within matrices (like $k$) is a standard QCAA technique to test algebraic manipulation and conceptual understanding of matrix operations simultaneously.
\end{takeaways}
