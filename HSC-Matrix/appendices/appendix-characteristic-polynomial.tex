\subsubsection*{Definition}
For a square matrix $A$ of size $n \times n$, the characteristic polynomial $f(\lambda)$ is defined as the determinant:
\[f(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I_n)\]
where $I_n$ represents the $n \times n$ identity matrix, and $\lambda$ is a scalar variable.

The roots of this polynomial ($f(\lambda) = 0$) are the \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues_and_eigenvectors}{eigenvalues} of $A$, which encode fundamental geometric and algebraic properties of the linear transformation represented by $A$.

\subsubsection*{Example}
Consider the $2 \times 2$ matrix $A$:
\[ A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \]

Construct $A - \lambda I_2$:
\[ A - \lambda I_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} \lambda & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 - \lambda & 1 \\ 2 & 3 - \lambda \end{pmatrix} \]

Compute the Determinant:
\[ f(\lambda) = \det\begin{pmatrix} 4 - \lambda & 1 \\ 2 & 3 - \lambda \end{pmatrix} \]
\[ f(\lambda) = (4 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) - (1 \cdot 2) \]
\[ f(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 12 - 2 \]
\[ f(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 \]

Setting $f(\lambda) = 0$ yields $(\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 5) = 0$, giving the eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 2$ and $\lambda_2 = 5$.

\subsubsection*{The Cayley--Hamilton Theorem}
A cornerstone result of matrix algebra is the \textbf{Cayley--Hamilton Theorem}: \emph{Every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation}. That is, if $f(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I) = 0$, then substituting the matrix $A$ in place of $\lambda$ yields the zero matrix:
\[
f(A) = O.
\]
For the matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ above with $f(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10$, this means:
\[
A^2 - 7A + 10I = O.
\]
We can directly verify this:
\[
A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 18 & 7 \\ 14 & 11 \end{pmatrix},
\]
and
\[
A^2 - 7A + 10I = \begin{pmatrix} 18 & 7 \\ 14 & 11 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 28 & 7 \\ 14 & 21 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 0 \\ 0 & 10 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = O.
\]

\subsubsection*{NSW HSC Heritage (1977 Q9): Computing Inverses via Cayley--Hamilton}
In the 1977 NSW HSC 4~Unit examination, students were asked:
\begin{quote}
\emph{The matrix $A$ is given by $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & -4 \\ -4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$.\\
(i) Write down the characteristic equation of $A$.\\
(ii) Prove that $A^2 - 10A + 9I = O$.\\
(iii) Find $A^{-1}$.}
\end{quote}
The Cayley--Hamilton relation provides a swift, cofactor-free method to compute the matrix inverse. Multiplying $A^2 - 10A + 9I = O$ through by $A^{-1}$:
\[
A - 10I + 9A^{-1} = O \implies 9A^{-1} = 10I - A \implies A^{-1} = \frac{1}{9}(10I - A).
\]
Substituting $A$ gives:
\[
A^{-1} = \frac{1}{9} \left[ \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 0 \\ 0 & 10 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 5 & -4 \\ -4 & 5 \end{pmatrix} \right] = \frac{1}{9} \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
This technique bypasses minors, cofactors, and adjugate matrices entirely!

\subsubsection*{NSW HSC Heritage (1970 Q6, 1975 Q7): Classifying Conics via Eigenvalues}
Symmetric $2 \times 2$ matrices $M = \begin{pmatrix} a & h \\ h & b \end{pmatrix}$ possess real eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ and orthogonal eigenvectors. In the 1970 and 1975 HSC examinations, students used these eigenvalues to rotate Cartesian axes and eliminate the cross-term $2hxy$ from quadratic equations:
\[
ax^2 + 2hxy + by^2 = \begin{pmatrix} x & y \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} a & h \\ h & b \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \lambda_1 {x'}^2 + \lambda_2 {y'}^2 = 1.
\]
If $\lambda_1, \lambda_2 > 0$, the locus is an ellipse with principal semi-axes $1/\sqrt{\lambda_1}$ and $1/\sqrt{\lambda_2}$ aligned precisely along the eigenvectors. This was how 4~Unit originally unified linear algebra with analytic geometry.
