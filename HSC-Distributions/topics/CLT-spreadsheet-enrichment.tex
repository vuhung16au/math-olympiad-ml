\subsection{Simulating the Central Limit Theorem (Python Lab)}
\label{subsec:clt-python}

\textit{This activity is a technology enrichment exercise and is not part
of the NESA Mathematics Extension~1 or Extension~2 syllabus. It uses
Python to illustrate the Central Limit Theorem by simulation.}

In this investigation, we use Python to simulate drawing samples from a strongly right-skewed population (an exponential distribution) and demonstrate that the distribution of sample means $\bar{X}$ is approximately normal, exactly as predicted by the Central Limit Theorem.

\textbf{Simulation Setup:}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Generate $10{,}000$ values from an exponential distribution with scale parameter $\lambda^{-1} = 20$. For this distribution, the population mean and standard deviation are both $\mu = \sigma \approx 20$, and the skewness is around $2$ (strongly asymmetrical).
  \item Repeatedly draw $M = 2{,}000$ random samples of size $n = 30$ with replacement, and calculate the sample mean $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$ for each sample.
  \item Plot the empirical distribution of these $2{,}000$ sample means using \texttt{seaborn} and overlay the theoretical normal density curve $N\!\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=pythonstyle]
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
import seaborn as sns
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# Step 1: Generate a right-skewed population (exponential, mean = 20)
population = rng.exponential(scale=20, size=10_000)
mu = population.mean()
sigma = population.std()

# Step 2: Draw 2000 samples of size n = 30 and compute each mean
n = 30
sample_means = np.array([
    rng.choice(population, size=n, replace=True).mean()
    for _ in range(2000)
])

# Step 3: Plot the histogram with seaborn and overlay the CLT normal curve
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4.5))
sns.histplot(sample_means, bins=40, stat="density",
             color="steelblue", alpha=0.6, ax=ax,
             label="Sample means (n = 30)")

# Theoretical CLT normal curve: N(mu, sigma^2 / n)
x = np.linspace(sample_means.min(), sample_means.max(), 300)
ax.plot(x, stats.norm.pdf(x, mu, sigma / np.sqrt(n)),
        color="crimson", linewidth=2, label="CLT normal prediction")

ax.set_xlabel(r"Sample mean $\bar{x}$")
ax.set_ylabel("Density")
ax.set_title(f"Central Limit Theorem in Action (n = {n})\n"
             f"Population Skewness = {stats.skew(population):.2f}")
ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Observations and Takeaways:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Emergence of symmetry:} Despite the population being severely right-skewed with a long positive tail, the histogram of sample means $\bar{X}$ is bell-shaped and symmetric about $\mu \approx 20$.
  \item \textbf{Narrower spread (standard error):} The spread of $\bar{X}$ is governed by $\mathrm{s.d.}(\bar{X}) = \sigma / \sqrt{n} \approx 20 / \sqrt{30} \approx 3.65$. Individual observations fluctuate wildly between $0$ and over $100$, but sample averages rarely deviate beyond $20 \pm 3(3.65) = [9.05, 30.95]$.
  \item \textbf{Generality of the CLT:} The original population did not need to be normal, or even symmetric. For moderately large sample sizes ($n \geq 30$), sample means behave according to a normal distribution regardless of the underlying population shape.
\end{itemize}
